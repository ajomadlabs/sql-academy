# Resume and Positioning

**Days 1–10 for the first version. Revised at Day 35 and Day 50 as skills actually land.**

---

## The positioning problem, stated plainly

Five years of tenure, but the depth behind it is thinner than the title implies. That creates a real tension, and the resolution matters:

**Don't inflate.** A resume claiming Data Engineering, cloud platforms, and advanced optimisation gets interviews she'll fail in round one. Failed interviews burn companies for 6–12 months and damage confidence that's already the bottleneck.

**Don't undersell either.** Five years of production experience at a large services firm is genuinely valuable. She has worked in real delivery teams, against real deadlines, with real stakeholders, in production systems. Most candidates with better SQL don't have that, and it's not a small thing.

**The honest position:** *An experienced software engineer with five years in enterprise delivery, specialising in SQL and database development.* That's true on Day 1, more true by Day 45, and it survives every follow-up question — which is exactly the property her resume needs right now.

---

## Days 1–3 — Excavate the real experience

Before writing anything, sit down together and dig out what she actually did over five years. This takes a couple of hours and is the most valuable part of the process — people systematically forget their own work, and the more self-critical you are the more you forget.

Questions that surface concrete material:
- What did you build or change that's still running?
- What broke, and how did you fix it?
- Which queries or jobs did you make faster? By how much?
- What did people come to you for?
- Which tables or modules did you know better than anyone else?
- What did you automate, or stop being manual?
- Who did you help or onboard?
- What did you catch before it reached production?

Write every answer down, including the ones that feel too small. "I rewrote a report query that was timing out" is a strong resume bullet. She will not think it counts. It does.

## Days 4–7 — Write it

**Format:** reverse-chronological, 2 pages maximum, ATS-safe (no columns, tables, graphics, or text boxes — they parse badly). Standard headings.

**Structure:**
1. Name, title, contact, LinkedIn, GitHub
2. Professional summary — 3 lines
3. Technical skills — grouped, honest
4. Experience — reverse chronological
5. Projects (added around Day 50)
6. Education, certifications

**Summary — target shape:**
> Software Engineer with 5 years' experience in enterprise application development and database engineering. Strong background in SQL development — complex queries, performance tuning, and data modelling — within large-scale delivery environments. Seeking a database/backend-focused engineering role.

**Bullets — the formula:** *action verb + what + technology + outcome.* Numbers wherever they exist; approximations are fine and expected.

Weak → strong:
- ~~"Responsible for writing SQL queries"~~ → **"Developed and optimised complex SQL queries across a 40+ table schema supporting daily operational reporting."**
- ~~"Worked on bug fixes"~~ → **"Diagnosed and resolved production data issues, reducing recurring defects in the reporting module."**
- ~~"Involved in database work"~~ → **"Tuned slow-running queries through index redesign and query rewrites, cutting report generation time significantly."**

**Skills section — the honesty rule:** list only what survives a follow-up question. Group them:
- *Databases:* SQL Server / Oracle / PostgreSQL, T-SQL/PLSQL, query optimisation, data modelling
- *Languages:* SQL, Python (add at Day 45, not before)
- *Tools:* Git, JIRA, SSMS, whatever she's actually used

Nothing goes on this list before the corresponding checkpoint in `01` or `02` is passed. That rule is what keeps the resume defensible.

## Days 8–10 — LinkedIn

- **Headline:** `SQL Developer | Database Engineer | 5 Years Experience` — not the internal job title, which means nothing outside the current employer.
- **About:** the summary, expanded to ~5 lines, first person.
- **Experience:** mirror the resume.
- **Open to work:** on, recruiters-only.
- **Skills:** add and order deliberately; LinkedIn's search ranks on them, and recruiters filter on them.
- **Activity:** follow relevant companies and recruiters. No need to post.

**One high-leverage detail:** recruiters search LinkedIn by keyword. If "SQL Developer" isn't in the headline, About, and skills, she won't appear in the searches that matter — regardless of how good the rest of the profile is.

## Day 35 and Day 50 — Revisions

**Day 35:** add SQL depth now genuinely held — window functions, performance tuning, data modelling. By this point it's earned.

**Day 50:** add Python, and add the project with a link to its GitHub repo. Rewrite the summary to lead with database engineering rather than general software engineering.

## Recurring objections, and honest answers

**"Why are you leaving after 5 years?"**
> "I've had good experience in enterprise delivery, but I want to specialise. My strongest work has been on the database side and I want a role where that's the core of the job rather than one part of it."

**"You've had a gap between projects — is that a concern?"**
> "It gave me time to invest properly in my technical depth. I've spent the last two months going deep on SQL — performance tuning, data modelling, window functions — and building projects. I'd rather use the time than wait it out."

That answer converts the weakest-looking fact into evidence of initiative, and it has the advantage of being true.

**"Rate your SQL out of 10."**
Say **7**, then qualify: *"Strong on complex queries, window functions, and optimisation; still building depth on large-scale performance tuning."* A confident 7 with specifics reads far better than a hesitant 9 — the hedge is what interviewers notice, not the number.
